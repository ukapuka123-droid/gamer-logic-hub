How Limbo works: multipliers and odds

Why does a 2× target hit often while 100× is roughly a one-in-a-hundred shot? One formula, a probability table and examples tell the story.

Published: September 14, 2026 · about 11 minutes

2×≈ 49.5% at 99% RTP
→
100×≈ 0.99% at 99% RTP
In short: you set a target multiplier before each Limbo round. The higher the target, the lower the chance of reaching it. In a simple model, probability = RTP ÷ target multiplier. Switching from 2× to 100× changes how often and how much you win; it does not turn a negative expected return into a positive one.

Limbo in plain English

Limbo is an instant multiplier game. Before a round you choose a target—say 1.5×, 2×, 10× or 100×. A random-number generator then produces a result. If the result reaches or exceeds your target, the stake is multiplied by that target. If it falls short, the stake is lost.

The main trade-off is frequency versus size. Low targets hit more often but pay less. High targets pay more when they hit, but long losing runs are normal. A flashy animation or rocket graphic does not change the underlying comparison between the target and the random outcome.

1.5×frequent, modest payout
10×less frequent, larger payout
100×rare, much larger payout

How to calculate Limbo odds

For a common fixed-RTP model, the chance of hitting a target is:

P = RTP ÷ X

P is the win probability, RTP is expressed as a decimal and X is your target. For a 99% RTP, use 0.99.

Example: 10× target.
0.99 ÷ 10 = 0.099, or 9.9%. That works out to about one hit per 10.1 rounds over a very large sample. It does not mean every tenth round must win.

At 100×, the same calculation gives 0.99%. Two 100× hits can appear close together, or none may appear for hundreds of rounds. Random results do not arrive on a tidy schedule.

Check the actual game rules: this table assumes a fixed 99% RTP. Another implementation may use a different RTP, rounding rule, minimum or maximum target, or outcome formula.

RTP, house edge and expected return

RTP is the theoretical share of stakes returned as prizes over a very large number of rounds. At 99% RTP, the house edge is 1%:

House edge = 100% − RTP

In the basic model the expected return stays the same across targets: 49.5% × 2 = 99%; 9.9% × 10 = 99%; 0.99% × 100 = 99%. The ride feels different, but the built-in edge has not gone anywhere.

For 100 hypothetical one-unit bets, turnover is 100 units and the expected return is 99. An actual 100-round session is very unlikely to land on exactly 99; short-run results can swing widely.

RTP is not personal cashback. A 99% figure describes a long-run mathematical property, not what your next 100 rounds owe you.

Why a streak cannot predict the next round

After ten low results, it is tempting to think a big multiplier is “due”. That is the gambler’s fallacy. If rounds are independent, your next 2× attempt still has a 49.5% chance in this model. A past 100× hit does not ban another one, either. RNG testing standards examine both the outcome distribution and statistical independence.

Do Limbo strategies work?

Low targets

Targets around 1.2–2× produce more wins and a smoother-looking history. Yet one loss can wipe out the gains from several small hits. A high hit rate is not a positive expected return.

High targets

At 50× or 100×, dry spells are part of the maths. A big hit looks exciting, but its rarity is already priced into the payout.

Martingale and chasing losses

Doubling after a loss does not improve the odds. It rapidly increases exposure; a losing streak, bet limit or finite balance can stop the system before any recovery.

Auto-bet and switching targets

Automation saves clicks, not probability. Switching from 2× to 10× after a streak changes the risk of the next round, but yesterday’s results do not make the new target more favourable.

“A big X is due.”Independent rounds do not keep score.
“1.1× is basically safe.”Small loss probabilities still show up over time.
“Changing my seed boosts my chances.”It changes the sequence, not the stated odds.
“More rounds will win it back.”More turnover increases exposure to the house edge.

Limbo versus Crash

Both mechanics make bigger multipliers rarer. In Limbo you lock in a target before an instant result. In Crash the multiplier usually climbs on-screen and you decide when to cash out before the round ends. The timing feels different; the rules and exact formula must still be checked for each version.

Frequently asked questions

Can I predict the next Limbo multiplier?

No. The formula gives a probability, not the next random outcome. A verifiable result can be checked afterwards, not called in advance.

Which Limbo target is best?

If RTP is constant across targets, none improves expected return. Lower targets hit more often; higher targets hit less often but pay more.

Is a big result due after a losing streak?

No. In a properly independent game, previous outcomes do not increase the chance of a big multiplier next time.

How is Limbo different from Crash?

Limbo settles against a target chosen in advance. Crash typically asks you to cash out while a multiplier rises.
