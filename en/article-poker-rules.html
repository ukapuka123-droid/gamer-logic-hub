Poker rules for beginners: how to play Texas Hold'em

Two cards in your hand, five on the table—and somehow the other player wins while you're trying to count “three pairs.” Let's walk through one hand from the blinds to the showdown, with the poker slang explained as we go.

Published: September 19, 2026 · about 12 minutes

PreflopFlopTurnRiver
The short version: in Texas Hold'em, each player receives two private cards and up to five community cards appear on the table. Make the best five-card hand from any five of those seven cards—or win the pot when everyone else folds.

What game are we talking about?

Poker is a family of games. This guide covers Texas Hold'em, the version most people mean when they ask how to play poker. It uses a standard 52-card deck without jokers. Players compete with one another for the pot, the chips committed during a hand. The dealer isn't your opponent, unlike in blackjack.

Your two private cards are called hole cards. The five face-up cards in the middle form the board and belong to everyone. You may use both hole cards, one, or neither. Only the best five-card combination counts.

Keep this in mind: “three pairs” isn't a final poker hand. If you can see three pairs among seven cards, your best five-card hand contains the two highest pairs and a kicker—the fifth card used to break a tie.

How a Texas Hold'em hand unfolds

Before the deal, the two players to the left of the button (the dealer-position marker) post the small and big blinds. These forced bets get the pot started. The button moves after each hand, so the positions rotate.

  1. Preflop: everyone gets two face-down cards, followed by the first betting round.
  2. Flop: three community cards are dealt face up, followed by another betting round.
  3. Turn: a fourth community card appears and players bet again.
  4. River: the fifth community card appears, then comes the final betting round.
  5. Showdown: if more than one player remains, the best five-card hands are compared. Equal hands split the pot.

If everyone else folds at any point, the last player standing takes the pot without showing their cards. At showdown, however, a great bluff story won't help: the cards decide.

Check, call, raise, fold: the table language

CheckPass the action without adding chips. You can do this only when there's no outstanding bet to match.
BetMake the first wager in a betting round when nobody has bet yet.
CallMatch the current bet to stay in the hand.
RaiseIncrease the current bet. The allowed amount depends on the game's betting structure.
FoldGive up your cards and leave this hand.
All-inCommit all the chips you have left at the table; with multiple players, a side pot may be needed.

A useful beginner check: if someone has bet, you can't simply check. Your choices are to call, raise, or fold. If nobody has bet, you may check and see what happens next.

These are the standard high-hand rankings for Texas Hold'em. A, K, Q and J mean ace, king, queen and jack.

A royal flush is the highest straight flush, usually given its own row on hand charts. An ace may be high in 10–J–Q–K–A or low in A–2–3–4–5, but Q–K–A–2–3 is not a straight. Suits have no ranking over one another.

What if both players have the same kind of hand?

First compare the category, then the card ranks within it. A pair of aces beats a pair of kings. If both have aces, compare the kickers in order. For a full house, compare the three-of-a-kind first, then the pair. For two pair, compare the higher pair, the lower pair, then the kicker.

Sometimes it's a genuine split. If the board is 10♠ J♠ Q♠ K♠ A♠, everyone still in the hand has the same royal flush. Hole cards can't improve it; the remaining players share the pot.

One hand, start to finish

You hold A♠ Q♠; your opponent holds K♥ K♦. The flop is Q♦ 7♠ 2♣. Your pair of queens trails their pair of kings. The turn is 9♥—no change. The river is A♦: now you have two pair, aces and queens, against their pair of kings. Your best five are A♠ A♦ Q♠ Q♦ 9♥, which beat K♥ K♦ A♦ Q♦ 9♥.

That's a nice river, not proof that the last card always saves you. A decision is made before you know the next card. One lucky or unlucky result alone doesn't tell you whether the decision was sound.

Common beginner mistakes

  • Treating a pocket pair as a guaranteed winner. The board can change everything.
  • Forgetting that the board is shared. A straight or flush on the table is available to everyone.
  • Ranking suits. A spade doesn't beat a heart just because it's a spade.
  • Playing every hand. Folding is a normal decision, not a personal defeat.
  • Confusing rules with profit. Knowing hand rankings prevents mistakes; it doesn't guarantee winnings.
My take: work through a few hands on paper or with play chips first. Once check versus call and flush versus straight feel obvious, the rules have clicked. Chasing a loss after a bad river is tilt, not practice.

FAQ

How many cards does a player have in Hold'em?

Two private cards plus up to five community cards. Your final hand uses exactly five, including zero, one, or two of your private cards.

What is the strongest poker hand?

A royal flush: ten through ace, all of one suit. It is the highest straight flush.

Can you win without a strong hand?

Yes. If all other players fold, the remaining player wins without a showdown.

Can you use all seven cards?

No. You select the best five-card hand from the seven available cards.

Does a flush beat a straight?

Yes. A flush is five cards of one suit; a straight is five consecutive ranks, not all of one suit.
